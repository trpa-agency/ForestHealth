## Indicator Overview

Subalpine conifer forest occupies the highest forested ground in the Tahoe Basin,
generally above 8,500 feet, where short growing seasons, thin soils, heavy snowpack, and
wind produce open, slow-growing stands. The type is dominated by whitebark pine, mountain
hemlock, western white pine, and lodgepole pine, with mountain mahogany on drier exposed
sites. These stands take centuries to reach late seral condition and recover slowly from
disturbance, which makes their extent and structure a sensitive measure of long-term
change. Whitebark pine in particular is under pressure across its range from white pine
blister rust, mountain pine beetle, and a shrinking climatic envelope as warming pushes
suitable habitat upslope past the top of the mountain. This indicator tracks the
condition of subalpine conifer forest in the Region.

## About the Threshold

### Relevance

Subalpine conifer stands are the Basin's least resilient forest type in the sense that
matters most: they cannot easily be restored. A mid-elevation mixed conifer stand can be
thinned and burned back toward reference condition within a few decades. A whitebark pine
stand that is lost takes centuries to replace, and the climatic conditions that supported
it may no longer exist at any elevation within the Basin. These stands also hold
disproportionate ecological value — whitebark pine seed is a high-energy food source for
Clark's nutcracker and black bear, and the stands stabilize soil and moderate snowmelt
timing on the highest slopes, which affects downstream water delivery. Tracking this type
is how the Region detects climate-driven vegetation change earliest.

### Human and Environmental Drivers

The dominant driver is climate. Warming temperatures compress the elevation band that
supports subalpine species and lengthen the season during which mountain pine beetle can
complete its life cycle at high elevation. White pine blister rust, a non-native pathogen
introduced to North America in the early 1900s, is the primary disease driver in
whitebark pine. Fire exclusion allows shade-tolerant species to encroach on subalpine
openings in some settings. Direct human disturbance is limited relative to lower
elevations because much of the subalpine zone falls within designated wilderness, but
recreation pressure at high-use trail corridors and ski area operations affect localized
stands.
